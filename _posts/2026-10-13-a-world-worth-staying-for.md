They say, in Persian,

“Zendagi az tu, marg az man.
Rahaati az tu, narahaati az man.
Khushi az tu, ghaam az man.
Hama chiz az tu, wale tu az man.”

Life be yours, death be mine.
Peace be yours, unrest be mine.
Joy be yours, sorrow be mine.
Everything be yours, so long as you are mine.

Perhaps that is where I first learned to love.

To give without counting,
to suffer without speaking,
to gather another's sorrows into my own
and call the weight a privilege.

I grew up believing
that love meant becoming the place
where another's pain could rest,
even if it left no room
for my own wounds to heal.

But tell me,
what is life if death finds you
before I have lived enough beside you?

What peace could I ever know
in a world that holds your absence?
And how could I ask you to smile
while sorrow quietly devours your heart?

How could I find you
when you have lost your way,
if I, too, am lost
in the darkness I carry for you?

I have always believed
that to love someone is to heal,
to become gentler with the world
because one soul has been gentle with yours.

And if the troubles of this life
do not soften beneath your beloved's smile,
if their laughter cannot make the burden lighter,
if their presence cannot persuade your heart
to endure one more difficult day,
then what kind of love
have we come to know?

I once carried pain
as though it were an old companion.
Death was no stranger to me;
I had made my peace with its silence.

I could look into the dark
without asking what lay beyond it.
There was little in this world
I was afraid to leave behind.

Then you came.

And somewhere within the ruins
I had mistaken for a home,
something began to bloom.

Your love took root in places
I had long abandoned,
and slowly, almost unwillingly,
I began to belong to my own life.

That was when I grew afraid of death.

Not of the dying,
but of the leaving.

Of departing a world
that had only just begun to feel like mine.
Of missing the mornings we have yet to share,
the conversations we have yet to finish,
the ordinary days I have not yet lived
but somehow already miss.

I feared becoming a memory
in a life I had only just learned to love.

And then I understood
what I had never found in the saying
I grew up knowing.

I had learned to offer you everything,
but I had forgotten
that I, too, was someone worth keeping.

What good is a love
that saves you from the storm
but leaves me drowning in it?

What meaning is there in taking your sorrow
if I must disappear to carry it?

I had mistaken selflessness
for the disappearance of the self.
I had thought that loving you meant
I could bear every wound
except the wound of losing you.

But now I know
that I must remain, too.

Not because I love you any less
than the boy who would have given you everything,
but because I finally understand
that my life is not the price
I must pay for yours.

So let peace be yours,
and let me find peace beside you.

Let joy be yours,
and let me learn to smile without guilt.

When sorrow comes for you,
I will not promise to take it all away.
I will sit beside you in the dark
until we remember where the light is.

Let everything be yours,
but do not ask me to become nothing.

For I want to give you my world,
not the ashes of one.

I want to love you
with all that I am,
not with all that I can sacrifice.

And if the old saying asks me
to give you everything,
then let me give you this, too:

My life.
My tomorrow.
My still-beating heart.

For I have spent enough years
learning how to endure the darkness.

Now, I want to learn
how to live in the light with you.
